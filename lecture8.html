Lecture 8 — Correlation

📊 Correlation — Definition, Formula, Types & Examples

What is Correlation?

Correlation measures the strength and direction of a relationship between two variables. It helps us understand how changes in one variable are related to changes in another.

Pearson Correlation Formula:

$$ r = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})} {\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} $$

Types of Correlation (Expanded with Examples)

1️⃣ Positive Correlation
When one variable increases, the other also increases.

Example: Study Hours (X) ↑ → Marks (Y) ↑ If students who study more get higher marks, then X and Y are positively correlated.

2️⃣ Negative Correlation
When one variable increases, the other decreases.

Example: Speed of Car (X) ↑ → Travel Time (Y) ↓ Higher speed reduces travel time → negative correlation.

3️⃣ Zero Correlation
No relationship between the variables.

Example: Shoe Size (X) and IQ Level (Y) One does not affect the other → no correlation.

4️⃣ Pearson Correlation
Measures linear relationship between two continuous variables.

Example: Height vs Weight analysis in a classroom using continuous numeric data.

5️⃣ Spearman Rank Correlation
Uses rank-order (non-parametric). Good for monotonic trends.

Example: Ranking students by performance vs ranking by attendance.

6️⃣ Kendall Tau Correlation
Measures concordant/discordant pairs—good for small datasets.

Example: Ranking judges’ scores for contestants.

7️⃣ Partial Correlation
Correlation between two variables while controlling the effect of another.

Example: Relationship between Exercise and Weight Loss after controlling for Diet.

Real-Time Applications of Correlation

1. Stock Market & Economic Indicators

Correlation helps see if NIFTY moves with inflation, GDP, or gold prices.

2. Temperature vs Electricity Usage

High positive correlation — as temperature increases, AC usage increases.

3. Study Hours vs Exam Scores

Generally positive correlation — more study hours lead to better performance.

4. Height vs Weight

Strong positive correlation in most populations.

5. Advertising Spend vs Sales

More marketing often leads to higher product sales.

6. Rainfall vs Crop Yield

Moderate positive correlation — agricultural output depends partly on rainfall.

7. Blood Pressure vs Age

Medical studies show positive association as age increases.

8. Traffic Flow vs Time of Day

Peak hours show clear positive trends in vehicle count.

9. Social Media Usage vs Screen Time

Higher usage strongly correlates with overall screen-time duration.

10. Employee Experience vs Salary

As experience increases, salary tends to increase — strong positive correlation.
