Lecture 3 — Measures of Central Tendency

Measures of Central Tendency

1. Definition

Measures of Central Tendency are statistical methods used to identify the center or typical value of a dataset. They summarize large datasets using a single representative value.

2. Uses

General Real-Life Uses

  • Summarizing student marks
  • Finding average salary of employees
  • Customer behavior analysis
  • Understanding product pricing

Uses in Biology

  • Average glucose level in diabetic patients
  • Mean bacterial growth rate
  • Average plant height in experiments
  • Common blood group distribution in a population

3. Types of Central Tendency

A. Mean (Average)

Definition

Mean is the sum of all observations divided by the total number of observations.

Real-Life Examples

  • Average electricity bill
  • Average marks obtained by students
  • Mean fuel consumption of vehicles

Biology Examples

  • Average enzyme activity
  • Mean glucose level over 10 days
  • Average weight of lab animals

R Code

values <- c(120, 130, 140, 110, 150)
mean(values)

B. Median

Definition

Median is the middle value of an ordered dataset. It is especially useful in skewed data.

Real-Life Examples

  • Median household income
  • Median house price
  • Median age of employees

Biology Examples

  • Median survival time of cancer patients
  • Median growth rate of plants with outliers
  • Median viral load where a few values are extremely high

R Code

values <- c(2, 4, 10, 50, 100)
median(values)

C. Mode

Definition

Mode is the value that occurs most frequently in a dataset.

Real-Life Examples

  • Most common shoe size
  • Most used mobile brand
  • Most common problem in customer complaints

Biology Examples

  • Most common blood group
  • Most frequent bacteria species in a sample
  • Most common flower color in a population

R Code

get_mode <- function(x) {
  uniq <- unique(x)
  uniq[which.max(tabulate(match(x, uniq)))]
}

values <- c(2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5)
get_mode(values)

D. Geometric Mean

Definition

Geometric mean is used for multiplicative data such as growth rates, ratios, and fold changes.

Real-Life Examples

  • Average rate of return in investments
  • Population growth rate over years
  • Average price index movement

Biology Examples

  • Fold change in gene expression
  • Bacterial growth factor
  • Geometric mean of viral load reduction

R Code

library(psych)
values <- c(1.2, 1.5, 1.8)
geometric.mean(values)

E. Harmonic Mean

Definition

The harmonic mean is used for data in rate form, such as speed, concentration, or reaction rates.

Real-Life Examples

  • Average speed over different distances
  • Average price per unit at varying quantities

Biology Examples

  • Mean clearance rate of toxins
  • Average PCR sequencing rate
  • Average enzyme reaction rates (reciprocal values)

R Code

library(psych)
values <- c(2, 4, 6)
harmonic.mean(values)

F. Trimmed Mean

Definition

Trimmed mean removes a certain percentage of the lowest and highest values before calculating the mean. Useful when dataset contains outliers.

Real-Life Examples

  • Sports scoring after removing extreme judge scores
  • Average income calculation removing very high outliers

Biology Examples

  • Removing abnormal sensor readings in heart rate data
  • Eliminating failed assays in enzyme studies
  • Averaging plant height after excluding mutant values

R Code

values <- c(10, 12, 13, 14, 100)
mean(values, trim = 0.1)
