Lecture 3 — Probability and Probability Distributions

This lecture introduces probability as the foundation of statistical inference, followed by probability distributions, the normal distribution, and standard scores (Z-scores) used for comparing and interpreting data.

1. Probability: Concept and Definition

Probability measures the likelihood of an event occurring. It ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

P(Event) = Number of favorable outcomes / Total number of outcomes

Examples:
  • Probability of randomly selecting a male participant from a group of 60 males and 40 females: P(Male) = 60 / 100 = 0.6
  • Probability of drawing a red blood cell from a blood sample where 70% are red: P(RBC) = 0.7
  • Probability of a patient developing a certain enzyme deficiency based on historical data: P(Deficiency) = Number affected / Total patients

2. Types of Probability

  • Theoretical Probability: Based on reasoning or known possible outcomes.
  • Experimental Probability: Based on observed data or experiments.
  • Conditional Probability: Probability of an event given another event has occurred.

    P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

3. Probability Distributions

A probability distribution describes how probabilities are distributed over possible outcomes of a random variable.

  • Discrete Probability Distribution: Random variable takes specific, countable values. Example: Number of patients with high blood pressure in a sample of 50.
  • Continuous Probability Distribution: Random variable can take any value in a range. Example: Blood glucose level of patients measured in mg/dL.

4. Normal Distribution

The normal distribution is a symmetric, bell-shaped distribution defined by its mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ).

f(x) = (1 / (σ√2π)) × e^(-(x-μ)² / 2σ²)

Characteristics of normal distribution:

  • Mean = Median = Mode
  • 68% of values lie within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ
  • Symmetrical around the mean
Examples:
  • Distribution of body temperature in healthy adults.
  • Serum cholesterol levels across a large patient population.
  • Height of plants in a controlled experiment.

5. Z-Scores (Standard Scores)

Z-scores indicate how many standard deviations a value is from the mean, allowing comparison across different scales.

Z = (X - μ) / σ

Examples:
  • A patient’s blood glucose of 150 mg/dL when the mean is 120 mg/dL and SD = 15 mg/dL: Z = (150-120)/15 = 2 → 2 SD above mean.
  • Comparing enzyme activity between two experiments with different units using Z-scores.
  • Standardizing patient body weights to detect outliers.

6. Summary

  • Probability measures the likelihood of events and forms the basis for inferential statistics.
  • Probability distributions describe how probabilities are spread across outcomes.
  • The normal distribution is key due to its symmetry and natural occurrence in many datasets.
  • Z-scores allow standardization and comparison across datasets with different means and SDs.
  • Biological examples such as blood pressure, glucose levels, and enzyme activity help illustrate these concepts.
