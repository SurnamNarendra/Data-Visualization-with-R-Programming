Lecture 6 — Introduction to Probability, Random Variables & Probability Distributions

1. Introduction to Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is to occur. It is a number between 0 and 1, where 0 means impossible and 1 means certain.

Basic rules (short)

  • For any event A: \(0 \le P(A) \le 1\).
  • Certainty: \(P(S)=1\) where S is the sample space.
  • If A and B are mutually exclusive, \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\).
  • Complement rule: \(P(A^c)=1-P(A)\).

Real-life examples

  1. Weather: Probability of rain tomorrow (e.g., 0.7 means 70% chance of rain).
  2. Medicine: Probability a patient responds to a drug (e.g., 0.6 success rate).
  3. Quality control: Probability a manufactured part is defective.
  4. Sports: Probability a penalty kick is scored.
  5. Genetics: Probability of inheriting a trait (Mendelian ratios).

Biology-related examples (5–10)

  • Probability a bacterial colony survives antibiotic exposure (e.g., 0.02 resistant).
  • Probability that a gamete carries a specific allele (Mendelian: 1/2, 1/4, etc.).
  • Probability a patient with a disease tests positive (sensitivity).
  • Probability an insect is infected with a pathogen in a sampled population.
  • Probability that a seed germinates under given conditions.
  • Probability of mutation at a particular nucleotide per replication cycle.
  • Probability that a cell enters apoptosis after treatment.
  • Probability a species colonizes a new habitat (colonization success).
  • Probability of survival to maturity for an organism (life table probability).
  • Probability that a PCR reaction yields detectable product (assay success rate).

2. Random Variables

A random variable (RV) is a numerical quantity whose value depends on the outcome of a random phenomenon.

Two main types:

  • Discrete RV: takes countable values (e.g., number of mutated cells).
  • Continuous RV: takes values in an interval (e.g., time until death).

Notation and examples

Common notation: \(X\) denotes a random variable, and we write probabilities like \(P(X=x)\) for discrete RVs or use densities for continuous RVs.

Real-life examples of random variables

  1. Number of infected patients in a clinic in one day (discrete).
  2. Time (in minutes) until a lab reaction reaches threshold (continuous).
  3. Number of mutations observed in a gene sequencing run (discrete).
  4. Weight of a sample of mice (continuous).
  5. Proportion of cells expressing a gene (continuous on [0,1]).

Biology-related examples (5–10)

  • Discrete RV examples:
    • Number of surviving larvae out of 20 (Binomial-type).
    • Number of bacterial colonies on an agar plate (Poisson-like).
    • Count of mutant individuals in a sample.
    • Number of reads mapping to a gene in RNA-seq (count data).
    • Number of cells showing fluorescence in a field.
  • Continuous RV examples:
    • Time until recovery after treatment (survival time).
    • Concentration of a metabolite in blood.
    • Length of leaves in a plant population.
    • Gene expression level measured as normalized continuous value.
    • Body temperature of individuals over time.

3. Probability Distributions

A probability distribution specifies how probabilities are assigned to values (discrete) or ranges (continuous) of a random variable.

Key functions

  • PMF (Probability Mass Function) for discrete variables: \(p_X(x)=P(X=x)\).
  • PDF (Probability Density Function) for continuous variables: \(f_X(x)\) with \(P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx\).
  • CDF (Cumulative Distribution Function): \(F_X(x)=P(X\le x)\).

Real-life examples

  1. Binomial distribution for number of successes in repeated trials (e.g., proportion of infected seeds that germinate).
  2. Poisson distribution for rare counts (e.g., counts of rare mutations per genome).
  3. Normal distribution for measurements with many small independent sources of variation (e.g., heights).
  4. Exponential distribution for waiting times (e.g., time until cell division in a simple model).
  5. Uniform distribution for a randomly selected point in an interval.

Biology-related examples (5–10)

  • Number of bacteria colonies on plates: often modeled by Poisson or Negative Binomial.
  • Proportion of individuals with a genotype: Binomial.
  • Distribution of body sizes across a population: approximate Normal after transformation if needed.
  • Time between infections in an epidemic model: Exponential.
  • Counts of reads per gene in RNA-seq: Negative Binomial (overdispersed counts).
  • Survival times of patients in a clinical trial: survival distributions (Weibull, Exponential).
  • Proportion of cells stained positive in microscopy: Beta distribution for continuous proportions.
  • Number of offspring per individual in ecological studies: often Poisson or overdispersed variants.
  • Mutation counts per coding region across samples: Poisson or Negative Binomial.
  • Latency periods of pathogens (time-to-symptom): Gamma or Weibull.

4. Types of Probability Distributions — Definitions, Formula & Applications

Discrete distributions

Bernoulli

Definition: One trial with two outcomes — success (1) with probability \(p\), failure (0) with probability \(1-p\).

PMF: \(P(X=1)=p,\ P(X=0)=1-p\). Mean: \(p\). Var: \(p(1-p)\).

Real-life example: Whether a patient responds to a single-dose vaccine (Yes/No).

Biology examples (5):

  • Single cell survives a stress test (yes/no).
  • PCR reaction yields product (success/failure) in one tube.
  • Presence/absence of a pathogen in a sample (screening test).
  • Cell expresses a marker (on/off) in a binary assay.
  • Germination of a single seed under controlled conditions.

Binomial

Definition: Number of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials with success probability \(p\).

PMF: \(P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k},\ k=0,1,...,n.\) Mean: \(np\). Var: \(np(1-p)\).

Real-life example: Number of infected plants out of 20 inoculated.

Biology examples (8):

  • Number of mutant offspring among \(n\) progeny (Mendelian predictions).
  • Number of successes in antimicrobial assays across replicate wells.
  • Number of embryos that develop normally out of a set.
  • Number of patients responding to a drug in a small clinical trial sample.
  • Number of pollen grains that germinate out of \(n\) tested.
  • Number of colonies with resistance marker out of plated cells.
  • Number of plants showing a phenotype after treatment among \(n\).
  • Number of larvae surviving after exposure out of \(n\) larvae.

Poisson

Definition: Models counts of events occurring independently in a fixed interval when events are rare and mean rate is \(\lambda\).

PMF: \(P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda} \dfrac{\lambda^k}{k!},\ k=0,1,2,...\) Mean=Var=\(\lambda\).

Real-life example: Number of mutations observed in a gene per sequencing run when mutations are rare.

Biology examples (8):

  • Number of mutation events per gene per genome.
  • Number of bacteria colonies appearing by chance in a field sample.
  • Number of insect bites per person in a fixed time window.
  • Number of rare pathogen detections in environmental samples.
  • Number of spontaneous cell division errors in a culture per day.
  • Number of transposition events detected in a genome region.
  • Number of sequencing errors per read (approximate in some contexts).
  • Number of tumors appearing in a model organism in a year (rare events).

Discrete Uniform

Definition: Finite set of outcomes equally likely.

PMF: If values are \(a,...,b\), then \(P(X=x)=1/(b-a+1)\).

Real-life example: Choosing a random chromosome (if all equally likely) from a small set.

Biology examples (5):

  • Randomly choosing one of several equally abundant species for a pilot observation.
  • Random assignment of equal treatments to experimental units when all choices are equally likely.
  • Selecting a random gene from a small curated list for follow-up.
  • Randomly sampling one of \(k\) cultures when they are identical.
  • Choosing a random locus from a small panel for genotyping.

Continuous distributions

Uniform (continuous)

Definition: All values in \([a,b]\) equally likely per unit length.

PDF: \(f(x)=1/(b-a)\) for \(a\le x\le b\). Mean: \((a+b)/2\), Var: \((b-a)^2/12\).

Biology examples (5):

  • Random time of day that an experimental sample is processed when processing is uniform across a shift.
  • Random selection of a position along a homogeneous fiber for measurement.
  • Selecting a random angle for orientation in a behavioral assay (if uniform).
  • Random placement of a marker along a uniform DNA construct in simulation.
  • Assuming uniform prior for a parameter in a simple Bayesian example (educational).

Normal (Gaussian)

Definition: Continuous distribution shaped like a bell curve, used for many natural measurements.

PDF: \(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\). Mean=\(\mu\), Var=\(\sigma^2\).

Real-life example: Distribution of heights in a large population (approx Normal).

Biology examples (8):

  • Body weight or length measurements in a well-mixed population.
  • Distribution of enzyme activity across individuals under controlled conditions.
  • Measurement errors around a true lab value (instrument noise tends to be Gaussian).
  • Distribution of blood pressure in a sample from a population.
  • Normalized gene expression values after suitable transformation.
  • Distribution of sizes of eggs in a bird species (approx Normal often).
  • Phenotypic traits influenced by many small genetic and environmental effects.
  • Residuals in many regression models (assumption of Normality for errors).

Exponential

Definition: Models waiting times between independent events occurring at constant rate.

PDF: \(f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x},\ x\ge 0.\) Mean: \(1/\lambda\), Var: \(1/\lambda^2\).

Biology examples (7):

  • Time until first infection event in a simple Poisson process model.
  • Time until a cell divides in a memoryless model.
  • Time until failure (death) for a simple lifespan model with constant hazard.
  • Time between arrivals of individuals to a trap when arrivals are Poisson.
  • Time until detection of a rare event in continuous monitoring (approx Exponential locally).
  • Inter-event times between mutations under a constant-rate assumption.
  • Waiting time to observe the first successful culture in high-throughput screening under certain assumptions.

Gamma

Definition: Family of continuous distributions generalizing Exponential (sum of k exponentials).

Useful for modeling waiting times that are sums of multiple exponential stages.

Biology examples (5):

  • Time until completion of a multi-step biological process (e.g., multi-stage infection).
  • Distribution of incubation periods that are sums of several exponential phases.
  • Modeling dispersed waiting times for cell cycle stages.
  • Modeling aggregate enzyme reaction times in serial steps.
  • Modeling rainfall amounts in ecohydrology (affects biological processes).

Beta

Definition: Continuous distribution on [0,1], flexible for modeling proportions.

Useful as a prior in Bayesian models for probabilities or to model proportions.

Biology examples (6):

  • Proportion of cells expressing a marker in microscopy images.
  • Proportion of infected individuals in a sample (when modeling uncertainty).
  • Allele frequency estimates in small samples (as a prior/posterior).
  • Proportion of time an animal spends foraging versus resting.
  • Proportion of target mRNA expressed relative to housekeeping genes (normalized proportion).
  • Modeling efficacy rate of treatments when bounded between 0 and 1.

Negative Binomial

Definition: Discrete distribution generalizing Poisson to allow overdispersion (variance > mean).

Commonly used for count data with extra variability, e.g., RNA-seq gene counts.

Biology examples (6):

  • RNA-seq read counts per gene across replicate samples (overdispersed counts).
  • Counts of parasites per host when aggregation occurs.
  • Number of insects captured in traps across variable locations.
  • Counts of bacterial colonies with patchy distribution on plates or fields.
  • Number of disease cases across regions with heterogeneity.
  • Modeling overdispersed mutation counts across genomic regions.

5. Quick R Examples

Copy these into R or RStudio to run.

# 1) Bernoulli (simulate 100 trials with p=0.3)
ber <- rbinom(100, size=1, prob=0.3)
table(ber)

# 2) Binomial probabilities (n=20, p=0.2)
dbinom(0:20, size=20, prob=0.2)

# 3) Poisson (lambda=2.5)
dpois(0:10, lambda=2.5)
ppois(3, lambda=2.5)   # P(X <= 3)

# 4) Negative Binomial (simulate overdispersed counts)
rnb <- rnbinom(1000, size=2, mu=5)
hist(rnb, main='NegBin sample', breaks=30)

# 5) Normal sample and plot
x <- rnorm(1000, mean=50, sd=5)
hist(x, main='Normal sample', breaks=30)

# 6) Beta for proportions (alpha=2, beta=5)
prop <- rbeta(500, 2, 5)
hist(prop, main='Beta(2,5)', breaks=25)

Tip: R's functions follow the pattern d* (density/pmf), p* (cdf), q* (quantile), r* (random sample) — e.g., dbinom, ppois, qnorm, rbeta.

6. Short In-class Quiz

Q1. A plate receives on average 0.5 contamination events. Which distribution is appropriate for modeling the count of contaminations per plate? Why?
Answer: Poisson(\(\lambda=0.5\)) — appropriate for rare independent events in a fixed unit.
Q2. You measure enzyme activity 100 times and the distribution looks bell-shaped. Which distribution might you assume for modelling measurement error?
Answer: Normal distribution — Gaussian errors are common for measurement noise.
Q3. True / False: The probability that a continuous random variable equals exactly 2.5 can be greater than zero.
Answer: False — for continuous RVs, point probabilities are zero; probabilities are for intervals.

Quick formula cheat-sheet

Binomial: \(P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\).
Poisson: \(P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\).
Normal: see main formula above.
Exponential: \(f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) for \(x\ge0\).
