Lecture 6 — Correlation and Regression Analysis

This lecture introduces correlation and regression, which are statistical methods used to examine the relationships between variables, quantify strength and direction, and make predictions.

1. Correlation

Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables. The correlation coefficient (r) ranges from -1 to +1:

  • r = +1 → Perfect positive correlation
  • r = -1 → Perfect negative correlation
  • r = 0 → No linear correlation

Pearson correlation coefficient:
r = Σ((X - X̄)(Y - Ȳ)) / √(Σ(X - X̄)² Σ(Y - Ȳ)²)

Examples:
  • Correlation between fasting glucose and HbA1c levels in diabetic patients.
  • Correlation between body weight and blood pressure in a population study.
  • Correlation between enzyme concentration and reaction rate in lab experiments.

2. Regression Analysis

Regression analysis is used to predict the value of a dependent variable (Y) based on one or more independent variables (X).

  • Simple Linear Regression: One independent variable.

    Y = β₀ + β₁X + ε

    β₀ = intercept, β₁ = slope, ε = error term
  • Multiple Linear Regression: More than one independent variable.

    Y = β₀ + β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + ... + βnXn + ε

Examples:
  • Predicting fasting glucose level (Y) from BMI (X) using simple linear regression.
  • Predicting cholesterol level (Y) based on age, BMI, and exercise frequency (X₁, X₂, X₃) using multiple regression.
  • Predicting enzyme activity (Y) from substrate concentration (X).

3. Assumptions of Regression

  • Linearity: Relationship between X and Y is linear.
  • Independence: Observations are independent.
  • Homoscedasticity: Constant variance of errors.
  • Normality: Errors are normally distributed.

4. Interpreting Results

  • Correlation: Direction (+/-) and strength (closer to 1 or -1) indicate relationship between variables.
  • Regression coefficients: β₁ represents change in Y for a unit change in X.
  • R² (Coefficient of Determination): Proportion of variance in Y explained by X.

    R² = 1 - (SS_res / SS_tot)

Examples:
  • A strong positive correlation (r = 0.85) between fasting glucose and HbA1c.
  • Regression equation: Glucose = 80 + 1.5 × BMI predicts average glucose based on BMI.
  • R² = 0.72 indicates 72% of variation in blood pressure explained by BMI and age.

5. Summary

  • Correlation quantifies the strength and direction of linear relationships between variables.
  • Regression allows prediction of a dependent variable based on independent variable(s).
  • Assumptions of regression must be checked for valid inference.
  • R² measures how well independent variable(s) explain the variation in the dependent variable.
  • Biological and medical examples demonstrate real-world applications in research and clinical studies.
