Lecture 4 — Sampling and the Central Limit Theorem

This lecture introduces the concepts of sampling, different sampling methods, the idea of sampling distributions, and the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), which is fundamental in inferential statistics.

1. Population and Sample

A population is the complete set of individuals or observations of interest. A sample is a subset selected from the population for analysis. Sampling allows us to make inferences about the population when studying the entire population is impractical.

Examples:
  • Population: All patients with diabetes in a city; Sample: 200 patients selected randomly from hospitals.
  • Population: All blood donors; Sample: 500 donors analyzed for hemoglobin levels.
  • Population: All leaves of a plant species; Sample: 50 leaves chosen for chlorophyll content measurement.

2. Types of Sampling Methods

There are several techniques to select representative samples from a population:

  • Simple Random Sampling: Every individual has an equal chance of selection. Example: Randomly selecting 100 patients from a hospital database.
  • Systematic Sampling: Selecting every k-th individual from a list. Example: Choosing every 5th patient visiting a clinic for blood sugar measurement.
  • Stratified Sampling: Population divided into strata, then samples drawn proportionally. Example: Sampling equal numbers of males and females to study cholesterol levels.
  • Cluster Sampling: Population divided into clusters, some clusters are randomly selected. Example: Selecting certain hospitals in a city and including all patients from those hospitals.

3. Sampling Distribution

A sampling distribution is the probability distribution of a statistic (like mean or proportion) obtained from multiple random samples of the same size from a population.

Standard Error of the Mean (SEM):
SEM = σ / √n

where σ = population standard deviation, n = sample size.

Examples:
  • Distribution of mean fasting glucose from 100 random samples of 30 patients each.
  • Distribution of average white blood cell counts from repeated random samples of 20 blood samples.

4. Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The CLT states that the sampling distribution of the sample mean will be approximately normal, regardless of the population distribution, if the sample size is sufficiently large (n ≥ 30). This is crucial for applying inferential statistics.

If X̄ = sample mean, μ = population mean, σ = population standard deviation, n = sample size:
X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n)

Examples:
  • Average blood pressure of 50 randomly selected patients follows an approximately normal distribution, even if individual blood pressures are skewed.
  • Mean enzyme activity from repeated samples of cell cultures approximates a normal distribution.
  • Sampling mean heights of plants from multiple plots follows normal distribution regardless of plant height distribution in each plot.

5. Importance of Sampling and CLT

  • Enables reliable inference about populations using sample statistics.
  • CLT allows the use of normal distribution for confidence intervals and hypothesis testing, even with non-normal populations.
  • Reduces cost and effort in data collection while maintaining accuracy.
  • Essential for designing experiments, clinical trials, and surveys.

6. Summary

  • Sampling allows analysis of a subset to make conclusions about the population.
  • Different sampling methods ensure representativeness and reduce bias.
  • Sampling distributions describe the variation of sample statistics.
  • The Central Limit Theorem ensures approximate normality of sample means, enabling inferential procedures.
  • Biological and medical examples illustrate the applicability of these concepts in research.
