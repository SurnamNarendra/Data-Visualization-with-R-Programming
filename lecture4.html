Lecture 4 — Measures of Dispersion

Measures of Dispersion

1. Definition

Measures of Dispersion describe how spread out or scattered the data values are around the central value. They help understand the variability, consistency, and reliability of a dataset.

2. Uses

General Real-Life Uses

  • Understanding variation in students' marks
  • Variability in monthly expenses
  • Differences in salaries among employees
  • Consistency of product quality in manufacturing

Uses in Biology

  • Variation in blood pressure across patients
  • Spread of bacterial colony sizes in culture plates
  • Variability in plant height in experimental plots
  • Differences in enzyme activity across replicates
  • Studying spread of glucose levels in diabetic subjects

3. Types of Measures of Dispersion

A. Range

Definition

Range is the difference between the maximum and minimum values in a dataset.

Real-Life Examples

  • Difference between highest and lowest temperature in a day
  • Range of salaries in a company
  • Minimum and maximum marks in a class

Biology Examples

  • Range of plant heights in a greenhouse experiment
  • Difference between highest and lowest blood glucose levels in patients
  • Maximum and minimum bacterial colony diameters

R Code

values <- c(10, 15, 20, 30, 40)
range(values)
max(values) - min(values)

B. Variance

Definition

Variance measures the average squared deviation of each value from the mean. Higher variance means higher spread.

Real-Life Examples

  • Variation in petrol prices across cities
  • Fluctuation in daily stock market prices
  • Consistency of students' test scores

Biology Examples

  • Variation in enzyme activity across replicates
  • Spread of blood pressure readings
  • Variance in cell sizes in microscopy images

R Code

values <- c(12, 15, 18, 22, 30)
var(values)

C. Standard Deviation (SD)

Definition

Standard Deviation is the square root of variance and indicates how far data typically deviates from the mean.

Real-Life Examples

  • Consistency of monthly bills
  • Variation in travel time due to traffic
  • Spread of prices for the same product in different shops

Biology Examples

  • Standard deviation of fasting glucose levels
  • Variation in heart rate measured by sensors
  • SD of plant growth over multiple treatment conditions

R Code

values <- c(10, 12, 15, 18, 25)
sd(values)

D. Coefficient of Variation (CV)

Definition

CV = (Standard Deviation / Mean) × 100.
Used to compare variability between datasets with different units or scales.

Real-Life Examples

  • Comparing variation in petrol price vs. milk price
  • Comparing consistency of two machines' outputs

Biology Examples

  • Comparing glucose variability between patients
  • Assessing experimental reproducibility in enzyme assays
  • Comparing plant growth variation across treatments

R Code

values <- c(20, 22, 25, 30, 35)
cv <- (sd(values) / mean(values)) * 100
cv

E. Interquartile Range (IQR)

Definition

IQR is the difference between the 75th percentile (Q3) and the 25th percentile (Q1). It measures the spread of the middle 50% of data.

Real-Life Examples

  • IQR of house prices in a city
  • Spread of students’ marks in an exam

Biology Examples

  • IQR of gene expression values
  • IQR of heart rate variability data
  • Spread of plant height after removing extreme values

R Code

values <- c(5, 7, 9, 15, 30, 32, 35)
IQR(values)

F. Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)

Definition

MAD is the average of absolute deviations from the mean. It is a robust measure of variability.

Real-Life Examples

  • Average deviation of students' marks from class mean
  • Deviation in daily expenses from monthly mean

Biology Examples

  • MAD of plant growth measurements
  • Deviation in blood pressure readings from average
  • MAD of bacterial colony diameters

R Code

values <- c(10, 12, 15, 18, 25)
mad(values)
