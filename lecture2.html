Lecture 2 — Descriptive Statistics: Central Tendency, Dispersion, Skewness, and Kurtosis

Descriptive statistics summarize and describe the main features of a dataset. They help understand data distribution, identify patterns, and provide a basis for further statistical analysis.

1. Measures of Central Tendency

Central tendency represents the typical or central value of a dataset. Common measures include:

  • Mean (Arithmetic Mean): The sum of all values divided by the total number of values.

    Mean (μ) = Σx / n

  • Median: The middle value when data are arranged in ascending or descending order.
  • Mode: The value that occurs most frequently in the dataset.
Examples:
  • Average heart rate of patients during a clinical trial (Mean).
  • Median fasting glucose level in a study of 50 participants.
  • The most common blood type among a population sample (Mode).
  • Average enzyme activity measured across multiple test samples (Mean).

2. Measures of Dispersion

Dispersion indicates the spread or variability of data. Higher dispersion means greater variability, and lower dispersion means data points are closer to the central value.

  • Range: Difference between maximum and minimum values.

    Range = Maximum - Minimum

  • Variance (σ²): The average squared deviation from the mean.

    σ² = Σ(x - μ)² / n

  • Standard Deviation (σ): The square root of variance; shows spread in the same units as the data.

    σ = √(Σ(x - μ)² / n)

  • Coefficient of Variation (CV): Standard deviation expressed as a percentage of the mean.

    CV (%) = (σ / μ) × 100

Examples:
  • Range of blood pressure readings in a patient group.
  • Variance and standard deviation of cholesterol levels across 30 participants.
  • Coefficient of variation of hemoglobin concentration among samples to compare variability relative to mean.
  • Standard deviation of cell count measurements in multiple culture dishes.

3. Skewness

Skewness measures the asymmetry of the data distribution around the mean:

  • Positive skew: Tail extends to the right (mean > median).
  • Negative skew: Tail extends to the left (mean < median).
  • Zero skew: Symmetric distribution (mean ≈ median ≈ mode).

Skewness = (Σ(x - μ)³ / n) / σ³

Examples:
  • Positive skew: Most patients have low white blood cell counts, few have extremely high counts.
  • Negative skew: Most participants have high enzyme levels, few have extremely low levels.
  • Zero skew: Daily body temperature readings of a healthy population forming a symmetric distribution.

4. Kurtosis

Kurtosis measures the “peakedness” or flatness of a distribution:

  • Leptokurtic: High peak, heavy tails (kurtosis > 3).
  • Platykurtic: Flat peak, light tails (kurtosis < 3).
  • Mesokurtic: Moderate peak, similar to normal distribution (kurtosis ≈ 3).

Kurtosis = (Σ(x - μ)⁴ / n) / σ⁴

Examples:
  • Leptokurtic: Blood glucose levels in diabetic patients where most values cluster near the mean but extremes exist.
  • Platykurtic: Height of seedlings in an experimental plot showing wide variation with no sharp peak.
  • Mesokurtic: Normally distributed body temperature measurements in a healthy population.

5. Summary

  • Central tendency (mean, median, mode) identifies typical values in a dataset.
  • Dispersion (range, variance, standard deviation, CV) describes variability.
  • Skewness quantifies asymmetry; kurtosis measures peakedness of the distribution.
  • Descriptive statistics are essential to understand data patterns before applying inferential methods.
  • Practical examples in biology help relate abstract measures to real-world data variability.
